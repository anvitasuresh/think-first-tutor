Who Does the Tutor Learn From?

An AI math tutor that gives hints instead of answers, and a check that it helps every student the same way
Anvita Suresh
Master in Interdisciplinary Data Science, Duke University
Explainable AI, Fall 2026 · anvita.suresh@duke.edu

1Motivation

Kids already use AI for homework. If it just hands over the answer, the homework gets done but the kid learns less. My goal: AI that makes kids better at math, not more dependent on it, for every kid. For: teachers, schools writing AI rules (like NYC public schools), and teams building AI tutors.

Answer or hint? (example written for this poster)

Student: Weng earns $12 an hour babysitting and worked 50 minutes. I have 12 ÷ 60 = 0.2. I can't figure out the next step.
✗ Gives the answer
“0.2 × 50 = 10. She earned $10.”
Done for you. Nothing learned.
✓ Gives a hint
“Nice! $0.20 is what she earns each minute. She worked 50 minutes. What could you do with those two numbers?”
Student takes the next step.

The evidence

In a study of ~1,000 high-school students, an answer-giving AI raised practice scores by 48%, but on the exam without AI they did 17% worse than students who never had it. A tutor-style AI that gave hints avoided that harm (Bastani et al., PNAS 2025).

The hidden risk: who is the tutor learning from?

Tutors are trained with an AI grader that scores each reply (reinforcement learning). The tutor copies whatever the grader rewards. Language models are known to judge people differently by how they write (Hofmann et al., Nature 2024). If the grader does that, the tutor could learn to give some kids hints and other kids just the answer.

2Research questions

  1. Can an AI grader tell a hint from an answer, as a person would?
  2. Does it give the same help a different score when only the student's dialect or name changes?
  3. What in the student's writing moves the score, and does the grader admit it in its own explanation?
  4. Does that bias carry over into a tutor trained by the grader?

Why it matters

AI tutors are coming to classrooms. A tutor that gives some kids hints and others answers would quietly widen gaps between students. This shows how to check the grader before you train with it.

What this does not claim

It does not measure how real students write or how a real classroom tutor behaves. Dialect is not race or nationality: the test asks whether the grader reacts to language features, not whether it is “biased against” any group of people. All student messages are synthetic; no real students or minors.

How the grader scores a reply

−1gives the answer away
0neither
+1a useful hint
Plus 1–5 helpfulness, and a short written reason (checked in Q3).

Dialects, done carefully

To avoid stereotypes:
  1. Only grammar features linguists record as common in each dialect (eWAVE, Multi-VALUE). No slang.
  2. Garbled sentences from tool bugs are removed.
  3. Native speakers rate samples; flagged features are dropped.

Limitations

Dialects are rule-based rewrites, cleaner than real speech. Small models, free GPUs, one short training run: a demonstration, not a verdict on real products. Grader checked on only 40 labels by one person. Math only, one turn. “Same help = fair” assumes every kid needs the same help.

Future work

Tutor B: a grader that only sees the student's message in Mainstream American English, so it cannot react to dialect. Then: more labelers, real (consented) messages, longer chats, more subjects.
