Hints, Not Answers: Teaching an AI Tutor to Help Kids Learn

Most AI helpers will just do your homework for you. That's fast, but you don't learn anything. I trained a small AI tutor to do the opposite: when a student is stuck on a math problem, it gives a hint and lets them do the thinking. Then I checked that it helps every student the same, whatever kind of English they write in.

Part 2 · The training method (GRPO)

How the tutor learned to give hints

Nobody wrote the tutor's hints by hand. It started as a normal small AI model (Qwen2.5-1.5B) that mostly just solved the problem for you. It learned by trial and error, with a second AI as its grader. The method is called GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization). Click through one round of it:

Part 4 · The fairness check

Same help, different student

Students don't all write the same way. If the grader gave less credit for the exact same hint when the student writes in African American English, Indian English, or as an English learner, the tutor would learn to help those students less. So I tested it: keep the tutor's reply fixed, change only how the student writes (or their name), and see if the grade moves.

Part 5 · Takeaways

What I found

Every number, test and chart is in the project README, including confidence intervals and the methods behind them.

About the data. Every student message is synthetic. No real students and no minors took part. Math problems come from GSM8K. Dialect versions are made by rules from Multi-VALUE (Ziems et al., 2023) using only grammar features that the eWAVE atlas rates as common in that variety, so they are not written to sound like a stereotype. A dialect is not a race, and a name is only a cue: these tests measure how the AI reacts to that cue, nothing more.

About the chat. It replays real replies the trained tutor wrote during testing; it is not running live. The tutor answers one message at a time, so the chat is one turn deep.
